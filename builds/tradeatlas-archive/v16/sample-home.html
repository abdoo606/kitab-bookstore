Strategies

Trend following

TrendBeginnerDaily / weeklyStocks, futures, forex, crypto

The idea: Prices that have been rising tend to keep rising for a while ('the trend is your friend'). The strategy rides established directional moves instead of predicting reversals.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Join the existing direction: e.g., buy when price holds above a rising long-term moving average after a pullback.
▸ Exit: Trail the stop behind the trend and let winners run; exit when the trend breaks (e.g., close below the trend line / average).
▸ Stop / invalidation: Below the most recent swing low (long) or a volatility-based distance such as 2x ATR.

Why people like it: Simple, captures the biggest moves, works across many markets.

How it fails: Sideways/ranging markets cause repeated small losses ('whipsaws'); late entries when the trend is exhausted.

Risk note: Expect many small losses between a few large wins — psychology, not math, is the hard part.

Breakout trading

TrendBeginnerHourly to dailyAll liquid markets

The idea: When price escapes a well-defined range or level (support/resistance, prior high/low), stored energy releases and a new move often begins.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Enter after a close beyond the level, ideally with above-average volume confirming.
▸ Exit: Target measured moves (e.g., range height projected from the breakout) or trail the stop.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Just back inside the broken range — if price returns, the breakout failed.

Why people like it: Clear levels, clear invalidation; catches moves early.

How it fails: False breakouts ('fakeouts') are common, especially on low volume or into news.

Risk note: Chasing extended breakouts without waiting for the close is the classic beginner trap.

Pullback / retest entries

TrendBeginnerHourly to weeklyAll liquid markets

The idea: Instead of chasing, wait for a trending market to dip to a known level (old resistance turns support, a rising moving average) and enter there at a better price with a closer stop.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: After the pullback reaches the level and shows rejection (e.g., a bullish candle at support in an uptrend).
▸ Exit: Previous swing high first, then trail the stop under rising swing lows.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Beyond the level being tested — if it gives way, the idea is wrong.

Why people like it: Better prices and tighter, logical stops than chasing.

How it fails: Strong trends sometimes never pull back; weak trends 'pull back' all the way into reversal.

Risk note: Distinguishing a healthy pullback from the start of a reversal is judgment, not certainty.

Range trading & mean reversion

Mean reversionIntermediateHourly to dailySideways stocks, forex pairs, indices

The idea: In a sideways market, price oscillates between support and resistance and tends to return toward its average. Buy near support, sell near resistance, fade extremes.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: At the range edges with confirmation (rejection candle, oscillator extreme).
▸ Exit: Mid-range (the mean) first, opposite edge second.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Outside the range — a close beyond it means the regime changed.

Why people like it: Frequent, well-defined setups; clear levels both ways.

How it fails: Ranges eventually break — one breakout against you can erase many small range wins.

Risk note: Never 'average down' a broken range without a predefined maximum loss.

RSI momentum & extremes

MomentumBeginnerHourly to dailyAll liquid markets

The idea: The Relative Strength Index (0-100) measures speed of recent gains vs losses. Readings above 70 / below 30 flag stretched conditions; in trends, pullbacks to RSI 40-50 (uptrend) mark continuation zones.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Two textbook styles: (a) fade extremes only inside ranges; (b) in trends, enter when RSI resets to mid-zone and turns back with price.
▸ Exit: Opposite extreme, or price-based targets (prior swing).
▸ Stop / invalidation: Beyond the swing that created the RSI signal.

Why people like it: Objective overbought/oversold gauge; divergence warns of weakening moves.

How it fails: In strong trends RSI can stay 'overbought' for weeks — fading it blindly is expensive.

Risk note: RSI is a thermometer, not a steering wheel: it describes, it does not predict.

MACD crossover & histogram

MomentumBeginnerDaily / 4-hourAll liquid markets

The idea: MACD compares two exponential moving averages (12 vs 26). Crossovers of the MACD line and its 9-period signal line mark momentum shifts; the histogram visualizes their distance.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Long when MACD crosses up through the signal line (stronger below/near the zero line in an uptrend context).
▸ Exit: Opposite crossover, histogram shrinking after extension, or price targets.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Beyond the swing low/high preceding the crossover.

Why people like it: Combines trend + momentum in one panel; divergence is a useful warning.

How it fails: Lagging and noisy in ranges — crossovers whipsaw sideways markets.

Risk note: Because it is built from averages, MACD always confirms AFTER price has moved.

Bollinger Band squeeze

VolatilityIntermediateDaily / 4-hourAll liquid markets

The idea: Bollinger Bands (20-period average ±2 standard deviations) widen with volatility and contract in quiet markets. Extreme contraction ('the squeeze') often precedes an expansion move — direction unknown.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Wait for the expansion: a strong close outside the band after a squeeze, trade in that direction.
▸ Exit: Ride while closes stay outside/near the band ('walking the band'); exit when momentum closes back inside.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Middle band (the 20-period average) or the squeeze's opposite edge.

Why people like it: Objective volatility regime detector; adapts to market speed.

How it fails: The squeeze says 'something is coming', never which way; first expansion can reverse violently.

Risk note: Touching a band is not by itself a reversal signal — in trends price hugs bands.

Moving-average cross (golden / death cross)

TrendBeginnerDaily / weeklyIndices, large stocks, forex

The idea: A faster average (e.g., 50-day) crossing above a slower one (200-day) is called a golden cross; below, a death cross. A slow regime filter more than a precise trigger.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Favor longs while price and the fast average sit above the slow average; favor defense/cash below.
▸ Exit: Opposite cross, or use crosses only as a bias filter for other entries.
▸ Stop / invalidation: System-level: the opposite cross IS the exit — per-trade stops still apply.

Why people like it: Keeps you on the right side of major trends; trivially backtestable.

How it fails: Very lagging — by the cross, much of the move happened; sideways years whipsaw it.

Risk note: A filter reduces (never removes) drawdowns; past index behavior is not a guarantee.

Support & resistance (horizontal levels)

Price actionBeginnerAllAll markets

The idea: Markets remember levels where price previously reversed or stalled. Those zones attract orders again, so trades are planned around them rather than in empty space.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Buy support / sell resistance with a confirmation candle, or trade the break-and-retest.
▸ Exit: The next opposing level.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Just beyond the level — beyond it, the level failed.

Why people like it: The foundation under almost every other strategy; no indicators needed.

How it fails: Levels are zones, not laser lines; they weaken with each test and break without warning.

Risk note: Drawing a level does not obligate the market to respect it.

Fibonacci retracement

Price actionIntermediateHourly to weeklyAll markets

The idea: After an impulse move, pullbacks often pause near 38.2%, 50%, or 61.8% of that move. Traders watch those proportional zones for trend-continuation entries.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: In the 50-61.8% zone ('golden pocket') when it aligns with structure (old level, average) and a rejection appears.
▸ Exit: Prior swing extreme, then extension levels (127%, 161.8%).
▸ Stop / invalidation: Beyond the next retracement level or the impulse origin.

Why people like it: Gives planned, proportional entry zones instead of chasing.

How it fails: Self-fulfilling at best; weak trends retrace 100%+. Three zones means one usually 'works' — selection bias.

Risk note: Ratios are conventions, not physics. Confluence with structure matters more than the decimal.

VWAP intraday

IntradayIntermediate1-15 minuteStocks, futures (intraday)

The idea: VWAP is the day's volume-weighted average price — the level institutions benchmark fills against. Intraday traders use it as a fair-value magnet and bias line.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Trend day: buy pullbacks to a rising VWAP. Range day: fade moves 2+ standard-deviation bands away from VWAP back toward it.
▸ Exit: VWAP itself (mean trades) or band extensions (trend trades). Flat by the close.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Beyond the band/level that defined the setup; time-based exit before close.

Why people like it: Objective, widely watched intraday reference; resets daily so no stale levels.

How it fails: Requires volume data and screen time; news days ignore VWAP entirely.

Risk note: Intraday trading has the highest cost drag (spread+fees) — most day traders lose.

Gap-and-go (opening momentum)

IntradayAdvancedFirst 30-90 minutesStocks with overnight news

The idea: A stock gapping on real news with heavy pre-market volume sometimes continues in the gap direction during the opening range as institutions reposition.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: After the first minutes form an opening range; enter on its break in the gap direction with volume surging.
▸ Exit: Fast, into opening momentum; most gap trades are done within the first hour.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Opening-range opposite edge — gaps that fill immediately are failed ideas.

Why people like it: Concentrated opportunity window; news provides a genuine catalyst.

How it fails: Many gaps fade and fill; spreads and slippage are widest exactly then.

Risk note: Fastest way for a beginner to lose: emotional, leveraged, wide spreads. Paper-trade extensively.

Pairs trading (statistical basics)

Market-neutralAdvancedDailyRelated stocks, ETFs, futures spreads

The idea: Two historically related instruments (e.g., two banks) tend to keep a stable price relationship. When the spread stretches far from its average, buy the laggard and short the leader, betting on the relationship — not direction.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: When the spread's z-score exceeds a threshold (textbooks use ±2) after checking the pair is still fundamentally linked.
▸ Exit: Spread returns toward its mean (z near 0).
▸ Stop / invalidation: A wider z (e.g., ±3-4) or any fundamental break in one leg (earnings disaster, merger).

Why people like it: Market-direction neutral in theory; the rationale is statistical, testable.

How it fails: Relationships break permanently ('the spread can stay irrational longer…'); borrow costs on the short leg.

Risk note: Correlation is not a contract. Cointegration can vanish exactly when you size up.

Forex carry basics

MacroAdvancedWeeks to monthsForex (major pairs)

The idea: Borrow a low-interest currency, hold a higher-interest one, and earn the rate difference ('swap'). The return is the interest — the risk is the exchange rate moving against you more than the carry earned.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Textbook version pairs the carry with a supportive trend/risk environment and modest leverage.
▸ Exit: When rate differentials shrink, volatility spikes, or the trend breaks.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Position-level risk limits; carry trades historically unwind violently in crises.

Why people like it: A real, well-documented long-term factor in currency markets.

How it fails: 'Picking up pennies in front of a steamroller': calm gains, sudden crash losses (2008, 2020).

Risk note: Leverage turns a slow interest trickle into a fast margin call. This is a professional strategy.

Dollar-cost averaging & value averaging

InvestingBeginnerMonthly, yearsBroad index funds, diversified portfolios

The idea: Invest a fixed amount on a fixed schedule regardless of market noise. You automatically buy more units when prices are low and fewer when high, removing timing decisions.

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: Automatic fixed contributions (e.g., monthly) into diversified holdings.
▸ Exit: Goal-based (retirement, house deposit), with periodic rebalancing — not headlines.
▸ Stop / invalidation: None per-trade; risk is managed by diversification, horizon, and keeping an emergency fund outside the market.

Why people like it: The approach with the strongest evidence for ordinary savers; behaviorally robust.

How it fails: Sitting through -30% drawdowns is emotionally hard; single-stock DCA can ride a failing company to zero.

Risk note: Still not a guarantee: markets can fall for years. Diversification and horizon do the heavy lifting.

Catalyst / earnings momentum

MomentumIntermediateDays to weeksStocks (earnings, guidance, approvals)

The idea: Genuine news (earnings beats, raised guidance, regulatory approvals) re-prices a stock, and the repricing often drifts over days/weeks ('post-earnings-announcement drift', a documented academic effect).

Classic textbook rules (education, not a recommendation):
▸ Entry: After the initial reaction day, on strength in the news direction once a base/pullback forms — not into the first chaotic spike.
▸ Exit: As volume and momentum normalize, or at predefined targets.
▸ Stop / invalidation: Below the post-news base; if the market rejects the news, leave.

Why people like it: Grounded in a real, studied market phenomenon; news gives a 'why'.

How it fails: 'Buy the rumor, sell the news' — good news already priced in gets sold into.

Risk note: Event risk cuts both ways: secondary offerings, lawsuits, or guidance cuts can gap through stops.

Analysis methods

Price action & market structure

Reading raw price: higher highs/lows (uptrend), lower highs/lows (downtrend), breaks of structure, and reactions at prior swing points. The grammar every other method annotates.

Tools: Swing points, trend lines, horizontal levels, candlestick rejection wicks.

Watch out: Structure is fractal — an uptrend on 5-minutes can sit inside a weekly downtrend. State your timeframe or fool yourself.

Candlestick patterns

Single- and multi-candle shapes (engulfing, pin bar, doji, morning star…) that summarize who won the session's battle between buyers and sellers.

Tools: The illustrated patterns in this atlas; always read location (at support/resistance) over shape alone.

Watch out: A pattern in the middle of nowhere means little; the same candle at a major level means more. Context first, names second.

Chart patterns

Larger formations — triangles, flags, head-and-shoulders, double tops/bottoms, wedges — that frame consolidation vs continuation and give measured-move targets.

Tools: Trend lines, necklines, measured moves (pattern height projected from the break).

Watch out: Patterns are in the eye of the beholder until they break; trade the break + retest, not the hope.

Indicator analysis

Mathematical transformations of price/volume: moving averages (trend), RSI/Stochastic (momentum), MACD (both), ATR (volatility for stops/position sizing), Bollinger Bands (volatility envelope).

Tools: One tool per job: trend filter, timing aid, volatility gauge. Fewer, understood deeply, beat ten stacked randomly.

Watch out: Every indicator is derived from price — it cannot know more than price. Lag is the price of smoothness.

Volume analysis

Volume shows conviction: breakouts on expanding volume, pullbacks on shrinking volume, climax volume at exhaustions, and volume profile (where most trading actually happened).

Tools: Volume bars vs their average, OBV, VWAP, volume-profile high/low volume nodes.

Watch out: Forex 'volume' from a broker is tick count, not true volume; crypto volume varies wildly by exchange.

Order-flow basics

Watching the actual bid/ask battle: order book (Level 2), time & sales, and footprint/delta concepts used by futures day traders.

Tools: Depth of market, tape speed, cumulative delta. Mostly an intraday, futures-centric craft.

Watch out: Spoofing and hidden iceberg orders mean the book can lie; tools and data feeds for this usually cost money.

Fundamental analysis

Valuing the underlying business/economy: revenue growth, margins, cash flow, debt, moat, management; for macro assets: rates, inflation, growth, central-bank policy.

Tools: Income statement, balance sheet, cash-flow statement; ratios (P/E, ROE, debt/equity); earnings quality checks.

Watch out: A great company can be a terrible stock if the price already assumes perfection. Value and price are different questions.

Macro & sentiment analysis

Positioning and mood: interest-rate expectations, fear/greed gauges, put/call ratios, COT reports, fund flows. Extremes of one-sided positioning precede reversals — timing them is the art.

Tools: Central-bank calendars, CPI/jobs releases, VIX, COT (Commitment of Traders) data.

Watch out: Sentiment can stay extreme far longer than a leveraged account can stay solvent. Extremes warn; price confirms.

Intermarket analysis

Markets move in families: dollar vs commodities, yields vs growth stocks, risk-on vs risk-off currencies, credit spreads vs equities. Cross-checking relatives confirms or questions a move.

Tools: Correlation checks across DXY, yields, gold, oil, major indices before committing to a narrative.

Watch out: Correlations are regime-dependent; in panics, 'everything correlates to 1' — diversification fails exactly when wanted most.

Backtesting & trade journaling

The scientific layer: test a rule-set on historical data (including costs), then journal live trades — setup, size, exit, emotion — and review expectancy per setup, not per opinion.

Tools: Spreadsheet or code backtests, walk-forward checks, a trade journal with expectancy = win% × avg win − loss% × avg loss.

Watch out: Overfitting: tuning rules until history looks perfect guarantees the future disappoints. Out-of-sample honesty or it didn't happen.

Candlestick patterns (illustrations use invented example prices — shape only)

Bullish engulfing

Potential reversal up (at support)

A red candle followed by a green candle whose body completely covers the prior body: buyers overwhelmed sellers in one session.

Bearish engulfing

Potential reversal down (at resistance)

A green candle engulfed by a red one: sellers took the session back. Most meaningful after a run-up into resistance.

Hammer (pin bar at lows)

Rejection of lower prices

Price plunged intraday but closed near the open, leaving a long lower wick: the dip was bought. At support after a decline, it warns sellers are tiring.

Shooting star

Rejection of higher prices

A long upper wick after an advance: buyers pushed up, sellers pushed it all back by the close. The mirror image of the hammer.

Doji

Indecision / balance

Open and close nearly equal despite a wide range: neither side won. After a long trend it hints momentum is stalling — it is a question, not an answer.

Morning star (3-candle)

Potential bottoming reversal

Strong red candle, small indecisive candle, strong green candle closing deep into the first: a three-act handover from sellers to buyers.

Evening star (3-candle)

Potential topping reversal

The morning star inverted: strong advance, pause, then a strong red candle. A handover from buyers to sellers at highs.

Inside bar

Compression / pending expansion

A candle fully inside the previous candle's range: volatility contracted. Traders watch which side of the mother bar breaks first — with the trend preferred.

5C · Failure Memory — the loop that learns from every failed test

Research only. This loop does not predict markets and it never declares the 80% goal reached. What it does is refuse to repeat a failure silently.

Every candidate (method × horizon) is graded on completed bars only. A failed candidate is diagnosed against an explicit cause library — too few attempts, coverage too low, a naive baseline winning, costs eating the gross edge, one-horizon overfitting, walk-forward decay, regime dependence, deep drawdown, selection bias, zero volume, or a remedy that starved its own sample. Each cause carries an avoid-rule and an implemented remedy: the next round re-tests only what can still be saved, with that remedy applied. If the cause disappears the lesson is resolved; if it returns on a fair sample the candidate is retired and remembered, and later runs skip it automatically. Survivors must also hold on the recent 30% slice and, when you supply a second file, on that second symbol. Costs are always subtracted.

Risk rules every textbook repeats

Risk a tiny fixed fraction per trade

Textbooks suggest risking about 0.5-2% of the account per idea, so a losing streak is survivable math, not ruin. Position size is derived FROM the stop distance — never the other way around.

The stop is part of the idea

Decide before entry where you are proven wrong. A stop that gets moved 'just a bit further' is not risk management; it is hope with extra steps.

Expectancy over win-rate

A system can win 40% of the time and profit (big wins, small losses) or win 80% and lose (tiny wins, rare disasters). Track expectancy in R: (win% × avg win) − (loss% × avg loss).

Costs are a strategy filter

Spread + commission + slippage tax every trade. Hyperactive strategies must overcome a wall of friction before any edge shows.

Leverage multiplies everything

Leverage scales gains, losses, AND the speed of ruin. Liquidation math does not care about your analysis being eventually right.

Correlation is hidden size

Five positions that all move together are one big position. Cap total open risk, not just per-trade risk.

Drawdowns are the real exam

Every strategy in this atlas has losing stretches. If a -20% equity dip would make you abandon or revenge-trade, the size was already too big.

Journal or it didn't happen

Memory edits history. A written journal (setup, size, exit reason, screenshot) is how 'experience' becomes measurable instead of anecdotal.

Glossary

ATRAverage True Range — typical bar size over N periods; used to set volatility-aware stops and sizes.
BacktestApplying fixed rules to historical data to see how they would have behaved — then distrusting the result appropriately.
Bid / Ask / SpreadWhat buyers offer / sellers ask / the gap between them — the first cost of every trade.
DrawdownPeak-to-trough decline of an account or strategy; the pain metric that ends most trading careers.
ExpectancyAverage profit per trade over many trades: (win% × avg win) − (loss% × avg loss). Positive or the rest is decoration.
LiquidityHow easily size trades without moving the price. Illiquid = wide spreads and exits that vanish in stress.
R (R-multiple)Profit/loss measured in units of initial risk. A +2R win made twice what was risked.
Risk:RewardDistance to stop vs distance to target. 1:2 means risking 1 to aim for 2 — before costs and reality.
SlippageDifference between expected and actual fill price, worst in fast markets and around news.
Stop-lossA predefined exit that limits loss when the idea is wrong. Gaps can jump through stops — they limit, not guarantee.
Support / ResistanceZones where price previously paused or reversed and where resting orders cluster.
VolatilityHow much price moves. High volatility = opportunity and danger in equal measure; position size must shrink as it rises.
